# Don't Blink — treatment

Style: Pixel Countdown Science (`~/lemo-my-styles/pixel-countdown`). 1080×1920, 30 fps, ~34 s, English, no voice.

## Why it can go viral
A challenge the viewer takes part in from the first frame ("DON'T BLINK FOR 15 SECONDS"). Almost nobody makes it comfortably, so people comment their result and rewatch. The pay-off is a fact people will repeat ("you spend about an hour a day with your eyes shut and your brain hides it"). The share prompt targets a type of person everyone knows (a screen starer).

## Three structures
1. **Countdown challenge** (chosen). 15 → 0 with the eye drying out, then BLINK, then the facts the viewer just felt. This is the strongest hook, and the struggle is felt rather than explained.
2. **Count-along.** "Count your blinks for 20 seconds." Weak: people can't count their own blinks while watching.
3. **Myth on trial.** "Do screens make you blink less?" with a split verdict. True, but it opens on a question instead of an action. Kept as one beat inside structure 1.

## Cast, setting, prop
- **Hero:** a tear drop with a face, sitting on the lower eyelid. It "is" the tear film: it sweats, shrinks and panics as the eye dries.
- **Setting:** a giant open eye filling the middle of the frame (almond lids with lashes, white sclera, teal iris, a pupil with a glint). It's set in a deep-violet face ground with dithered pores and dust.
- **Prop:** a horizontal TEAR FILM gauge that drains over about 10 s.
- **Mood ground:** deep violet. Yellow only for the counter and key words.

## Facts and sources
- Normal blink rate is ~15–20 per minute (Bentivoglio et al., *Movement Disorders* 1997: ~17/min at rest).
- The tear film normally breaks up after ~10 s without blinking (tear break-up time: under 10 s is considered abnormal; clinical ophthalmology standard).
- About 1 hour a day with eyes closed by blinks: ~15/min × 16 waking h × ~0.25 s ≈ 1 h (blink duration 100–400 ms; Kwon et al. 2013).
- The brain suppresses blink perception (Volkmann et al., *Science* 1980, "blink suppression"; Bristow et al. 2005).
- On screens the blink rate drops by about half or more (Patel et al. 1991; Tsubota & Nakamori, *NEJM* 1993).
