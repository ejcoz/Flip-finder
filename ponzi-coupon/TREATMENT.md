# The Coupon — treatment

A 35-second explainer in the **Vintage Engraving Collage** style: Charles Ponzi's postal reply coupon scheme, Boston, 1920. English voice-over (Kokoro), English subtitles, 1920×1080 at 24 fps.

## 1. Three candidate structures

1. **The object tells its story.** A single International Reply Coupon is the protagonist. It falls onto the table, travels from Italy to Boston, gets multiplied into a pile of money, turns over and is shown to be empty, and is finally cancelled with a postmark. The story stays on paper the whole time.
2. **A portrait from clippings.** Ponzi's face is assembled piece by piece from newspaper scraps (the immigrant, the promise, the crowd, the arrest). It is strong on character, but in 35 s the mechanism gets lost, and the mechanism is the point of a Vox-style explainer.
3. **The maths that breaks.** Open on 50% in 45 days and compound it until the numbers tear the paper, then reveal the coupon behind it. It's clever, but it opens on abstraction, not on an image, and the coupon itself (the hook) arrives late.

**Chosen: 1.** A Vox explainer works by holding up one strange object and asking how it worked. The coupon is that object. It's tiny, real and printed, so it suits a collage, and it gives the film a bookend: it lands with a thud at the start and is postmarked "VOID" by the scheme at the end.

## 2. Logline and arc

*A postal coupon worth a few cents became the most famous fraud in America, because nobody checked whether the coupons existed.*

- **Setup (0–10 s):** the loophole. A coupon is cheap in Italy and worth more in stamps in the US.
- **Rise (10–20 s):** the promise, 50% in 45 days, and the money pouring in.
- **Turn (20–26 s):** near-silence. The coupon turns over: there's nothing behind it. New money pays old investors.
- **Proof (26–31 s):** the signature shot. He'd have needed 160 million coupons, but only about 27,000 existed.
- **End (31–35 s):** August 1920, collapse. A postmark slams onto Ponzi's face, and his name stays on the scheme.

## 3. Benchmark

- **Vox history explainers (2015–2020 era archival collage pieces).** *Learn:* one idea per shot, a voice that sets up a question and answers it with an object, numbers shown physically so the viewer can feel the scale, a paper texture under everything, and highlight colour used for the one word that matters. *Don't take:* their logo, their typefaces, their yellow highlighter brand mark, their sound logo, or any of their shots.
- **Editorial paper collage title sequences.** *Learn:* stepped cut-out motion with overshoot, and a shape that becomes the next shot. *Don't take:* any specific layout.

## 6. Cue map

- 2/4 music-hall march, 120 bpm: one bar = 1.0 s, beat = 0.5 s.
- 0–4: near-silence, then a pizzicato tick-tock under the typewriter, with a timpani hit on the coupon's landing.
- 4–15: oom-pah in F major: tuba staccato on the beats, bassoon staccato on the off-beats, clarinet melody, glockenspiel on the stamps.
- 15–20: the same march with xylophone added and a snare driving the money drops, one bar of eighth notes before 20.
- 20–26: silence, then low clarinet and harp in F minor, sparse.
- 26–31: rising tuba and bassoon line in F minor, a timpani roll with the column, a crash at the pull-back.
- 31–35: a slow cadence, the postmark slam at 33.0 on a tuba + timpani hit, then a ring-out.

## 8. Type and subtitles

- Titles and labels: IM Fell English. Slips and data: Courier Prime. Numerals: a lining face (Courier Prime digits), because old-style figures read badly.
- Every word sits on a paper slip with a shadow. No floating type.
- Subtitles: English only, so a single cream typewriter slip sits bottom-left at the size of the style's upper line (about 40 px). That corner is kept clear.

## 9. Voice script (Kokoro, American male narrator)

1. In 1920, a Boston clerk found a loophole in the mail.
2. Buy this postal coupon in Italy for pennies; swap it in America for stamps worth far more.
3. Charles Ponzi promised investors fifty percent in forty-five days.
4. By summer, a quarter of a million dollars was pouring in every day.
5. But he barely bought any coupons. New investors' money paid the old ones.
6. Backing his promises would take a hundred and sixty million coupons. About twenty-seven thousand existed.
7. In August 1920, it collapsed. The scheme still bears his name.
